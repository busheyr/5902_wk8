Psychiatric Emergencies and Severe Hyperglycemic Crises

Lecture Overview

This lecture focuses on the emergency evaluation, stabilization, and disposition of severe psychiatric and hyperglycemic emergencies encountered in emergency medicine. Students will examine hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS), ketoacidotic syndromes, acute agitation, psychosis, mood disorders, geriatric psychiatric emergencies, emergency sedation, capacity assessment, and psychiatric disposition planning.

Emphasis is placed on recognition of dangerous presentations, patient and staff safety, identification of medical causes of behavioral changes, de-escalation strategies, emergency sedation, and continuous reassessment during high-acuity psychiatric and metabolic emergencies.

As the final week of Emergency Medicine I, this lecture integrates many of the concepts discussed throughout the course, including shock recognition, airway management, metabolic emergencies, altered mental status, toxicologic evaluation, and disposition decision-making.

Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS)

Key Concepts

HHS is a life-threatening hyperglycemic emergency characterized by:

  • Severe hyperglycemia
  • Hyperosmolarity
  • Profound dehydration
  • Minimal or absent ketosis

Patients frequently present later in the disease process than those with DKA and often demonstrate significant neurologic dysfunction.

Progressive hyperglycemia results in:

  • Osmotic diuresis
  • Severe dehydration
  • Electrolyte losses
  • Reduced cerebral perfusion
  • Altered mental status
  • Severe hyperglycemia
  • Dehydration
  • Weakness
  • Confusion
  • Altered mental status
  • Neurologic deficits
  • Tachycardia
  • Hypotension
  • Aggressive fluid resuscitation
  • Electrolyte monitoring
  • Glucose monitoring
  • Insulin therapy
  • Hemodynamic support
  • Neurologic reassessment

Key Differences from DKA

Finding DKA HHS
Typical Patient Type 1 Diabetes Type 2 Diabetes
Ketosis Present Minimal
Acidosis Significant Mild or Absent
Glucose Elevated Often >600 mg/dL
Mental Status Changes Variable Common
Dehydration Moderate-Severe Severe

Clinical Pearl

Neurologic dysfunction in HHS often reflects severe dehydration and hyperosmolarity.

Ketoacidotic Syndromes

Key Concepts

Ketoacidotic syndromes occur when insufficient insulin availability results in ketone production and metabolic acidosis.

Although DKA remains the most common ketoacidotic emergency, clinicians should recognize that multiple causes of ketosis and acidosis may exist.

  • Tachypnea
  • Dehydration
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Abdominal pain
  • Altered mental status
  • Metabolic acidosis
  • Electrolyte abnormalities
  • Severe acidemia
  • Respiratory fatigue
  • Progressive mental status decline
  • Dysrhythmias
  • Shock
  • Fluid resuscitation
  • Electrolyte monitoring
  • Cardiac monitoring
  • Acid-base assessment
  • Continuous reassessment

Clinical Pearl

Metabolic stabilization requires continuous reassessment because electrolyte abnormalities frequently evolve during treatment.

Altered Mental Status in Emergency Medicine

Key Concepts

Altered mental status is one of the most common and highest-risk presentations encountered in emergency medicine.

Behavioral changes may represent:

  • Metabolic disease
  • Toxicologic emergencies
  • Infection
  • Psychiatric illness
  • Neurologic disease
  • Medication effects

Clinical Pearl

The most dangerous mistake in psychiatric emergencies is assuming the patient has a psychiatric illness before excluding a medical cause.

Immediate Priorities

  • Airway assessment
  • Glucose assessment
  • Oxygenation and ventilation evaluation
  • Hemodynamic assessment
  • Neurologic examination
  • Safety assessment

Life-Threatening Causes

  • Hypoglycemia
  • HHS
  • DKA
  • Sepsis
  • Hypoxia
  • Intracranial hemorrhage
  • Toxicologic emergencies

Psychiatric Evaluation in the Emergency Department

Key Concepts

Psychiatric emergencies require simultaneous evaluation of both psychiatric and medical causes of presentation.

Not all agitation, confusion, hallucinations, or psychosis is psychiatric in origin.

Initial Priorities

  • Medical screening examination
  • Airway assessment
  • Suicide risk assessment
  • Homicide risk assessment
  • Identification of medical instability
  • Evaluation for intoxication or withdrawal

Important Medical Mimics

  • Hypoglycemia
  • Hyperglycemia
  • Sepsis
  • Hypoxia
  • Electrolyte abnormalities
  • Medication toxicity
  • Intracranial pathology

Clinical Pearl

Psychiatric symptoms accompanied by abnormal vital signs should be considered medical until proven otherwise.

Acute Agitation

Key Concepts

Agitation is a behavioral emergency that may place patients, staff, and others at immediate risk.

Rapid identification of the underlying cause is essential.

Common Causes
  • Psychosis
  • Substance intoxication
  • Withdrawal syndromes
  • Delirium
  • Metabolic emergencies
  • Psychiatric illness
Initial Priorities
  • Scene safety
  • Verbal de-escalation
  • Medical evaluation
  • Identification of instability
  • Continuous reassessment
De-Escalation Principles
  • Calm communication
  • Respectful interaction
  • Limit environmental stimulation
  • Team coordination
  • Maintain safe distance

Clinical Pearl

Verbal de-escalation should be attempted whenever safe and feasible before pharmacologic intervention.

Emergency Sedation

Key Concepts

Some patients require pharmacologic sedation to permit evaluation and treatment while maintaining safety.

Useful for:

  • Anxiety
  • Withdrawal
  • Sympathomimetic intoxication

Useful for:

  • Psychosis
  • Behavioral agitation

May be appropriate for severe agitation when immediate control is necessary.

  • Respiratory depression
  • Airway compromise
  • Hypoxia
  • Dysrhythmias
  • Hypotension

Clinical Pearl

Sedated psychiatric patients require the same physiologic monitoring as any other critically ill emergency medicine patient.

Suicide Risk Assessment

Key Concepts

Suicidal ideation is a common emergency department presentation requiring careful assessment and disposition planning.

Assessment Components

Evaluate:

  • Suicidal ideation
  • Intent
  • Plan
  • Access to means
  • Previous attempts
  • Protective factors

High-Risk Features

  • Active suicide plan
  • Previous attempts
  • Psychosis
  • Substance abuse
  • Lack of social support

Clinical Pearl

Risk assessment should focus on both immediate safety and future risk.

Psychosis

Key Concepts

Psychosis may result from psychiatric illness, substance use, neurologic disease, or medical instability.

Common Findings

  • Hallucinations
  • Delusions
  • Paranoia
  • Disorganized behavior
  • Agitation

Emergency Priorities

  • Ensure safety
  • Exclude medical causes
  • Evaluate for intoxication
  • Assess decision-making capacity
  • Determine disposition

Clinical Pearl

New-onset psychosis in older adults should be considered medical until proven otherwise.

Psychiatric Emergencies in Older Adults

Key Concepts

Psychiatric complaints in elderly patients frequently have underlying medical contributors.

  • Delirium
  • Dementia-related agitation
  • Depression
  • Medication toxicity
  • Psychosis

Older adults are at increased risk for:

  • Polypharmacy
  • Drug interactions
  • Delirium
  • Atypical presentations
  • Medical mimics

Clinical Pearl

Delirium should be considered a medical emergency until proven otherwise.

Capacity and Refusal of Care

Key Concepts

Patients may refuse treatment only if they possess decision-making capacity.

Capacity Assessment

The patient must demonstrate the ability to:

  • 1
    Understand information
  • 2
    Appreciate consequences
  • 3
    Reason through options
  • 4
    Communicate a choice

Clinical Pearl

Capacity is decision-specific and may fluctuate over time.

Psychiatric Disposition Decisions

Key Concepts

Disposition is one of the most important decisions made in emergency medicine.

Clinicians must determine:

  • Who can safely be discharged
  • Who requires admission
  • Who requires involuntary treatment
  • Who has ongoing medical instability

Psychiatric Admission Considerations

  • Active suicidal ideation
  • Danger to others
  • Grave disability
  • Severe psychosis
  • Inability to care for self

Medical Admission Considerations

  • HHS
  • DKA
  • Delirium
  • Persistent abnormal vital signs
  • Toxicologic instability

Clinical Pearl

Safe psychiatric disposition requires both psychiatric and medical stability.

Clinical Reassessment

Monitor:

  • Mental status
  • Glucose
  • Electrolytes
  • Fluid status
  • Neurologic findings

Monitor:

  • Airway
  • Respiratory status
  • Sedation depth
  • Temperature
  • Hemodynamics

Monitor:

  • Safety risk
  • Suicidal ideation
  • Capacity
  • Response to treatment
  • Disposition readiness

Key Principle

Patients with psychiatric and metabolic emergencies frequently deteriorate despite appearing stable during the initial evaluation.
