Section 1: Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS) & Complex Ketoacidotic Syndromes

Hyperglycemic emergencies span a spectrum from Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) to Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS). HHS is a life-threatening crisis defined by extreme hyperglycemia, hyperosmolarity, severe dehydration, and minimal to absent ketoacidosis.

Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

  • Absolute Insulin Deficiency
  • Ketogenesis & Acidosis
  • Glucose >250 mg/dL
  • Rapid Onset (<24 hours)

Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS)

  • Relative Insulin Deficiency
  • Extreme Hyperglycemia
  • Osmolality >320 mOsm/kg
  • Insidious (Days–Weeks)

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS): Residual endogenous insulin suppresses hepatic lipolysis and ketogenesis but cannot prevent peripheral insulin resistance and massive hepatic gluconeogenesis. Hyperglycemia typically exceeds 600 mg/dL (often >1,000 mg/dL), inducing severe osmotic diuresis, massive total body water deficits (8–12 L or 100–200 mL/kg), progressive pre-renal azotemia, and effective serum hyperosmolality (>320 mOsm/kg).

Ketoacidotic Syndromes Beyond Typical DKA

Seen with SGLT2 inhibitors ("flozins"), pregnancy, starvation, or severe hepatic dysfunction. Serum glucose is <250 mg/dL, but high anion gap ketoacidosis persists.

Occurs in chronic alcohol dependence following binge drinking and prolonged emesis/starvation. Characterized by low or normal blood glucose, high anion gap metabolic acidosis driven by β-hydroxybutyrate, and a high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio. Managed with isotonic saline, glucose, and thiamine (not insulin).

Mild ketoacidosis (HCO₃⁻ usually >18 mEq/L) caused by prolonged glycogen depletion and lipolysis.

Diagnostic Formulae for Hyperglycemic Crises

Effective Serum Osmolality:

Effective Osmolality (mOsm/kg) = 2 × Measured Na⁺ (mEq/L) + Serum Glucose (mg/dL) / 18

Calculated Corrected Sodium:

Corrected Na⁺ = Measured Na⁺ + 0.016 × (Serum Glucose − 100) (Use 2.0 mEq/L correction factor for glucose levels >400 mg/dL).

Section 2: Resuscitation Protocols & Osmotic Control in HHS

The mortality in HHS (10–20%) is significantly higher than in DKA (<1%), predominantly due to underlying comorbidities, advanced age, severe shock, and underlying sepsis.

Stepwise Resuscitation Protocol

1. Primary Fluid Resuscitation (Priority #1)

  • Administer 1–1.5 L of 0.9% NaCl (or balanced crystalloids like Plasmalyte) in the first hour (10–20 mL/kg/hr) to expand intravascular volume and restore renal perfusion.
  • Calculate corrected serum sodium. If corrected sodium is normal or high, switch to 0.45% NaCl at 250–500 mL/hr. If corrected sodium is low, continue 0.9% NaCl.
  • Osmolality Target: Decrease serum osmolality at a maximum rate of 3–8 mOsm/kg/hr (or reduce glucose by 50–75 mg/dL/hr). Rapid drops risk cerebral edema.

2. Potassium Management Prior to Insulin

  • Check serum potassium prior to starting insulin.
  • If K⁺ < 3.3 mEq/L, hold insulin and give IV KCl (20–40 mEq/hr) until K⁺ > 3.3 mEq/L.
  • If K⁺ is 3.3–5.2 mEq/L, add 20–30 mEq KCl per liter of maintenance fluid to keep potassium between 4.0–5.0 mEq/L.

3. Delayed Low-Dose Insulin Infusion

  • Unlike DKA, insulin administration in HHS should be delayed until initial volume expansion is underway.
  • Initiate IV regular insulin infusion at 0.05–0.1 units/kg/hr.
  • Once blood glucose reaches 300 mg/dL, add 5% Dextrose to maintenance fluids (D₅ ½NS) and reduce insulin rate to 0.02–0.05 units/kg/hr to maintain glucose between 250–300 mg/dL until hyperosmolality and mental status clear.

Section 3: Medical Mimics of Psychiatric Emergencies & Delirium Evaluation

A fundamental rule of emergency medicine is that all behavioral disturbances are considered medical (organic) until proven otherwise. Assuming an acute behavioral change is purely psychiatric without an organic screening evaluation is a critical error.

Section 4: Emergency De-escalation & Pharmacologic Sedation Protocols

When managing acute agitation, clinicians must prioritize staff and patient safety using a stepwise framework:

Staged Agitation Management Protocol

LEVEL 1: NON-PHARMACOLOGIC / ENVIRONMENTAL DE-ESCALATION

  • Verbal De-escalation (Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale +1 to +2)
  • Maintain distance, calm voice, offer choices, reduce stimulation

↓

LEVEL 2: ORAL VOLUNTARY PHARMACOLOGIC DE-ESCALATION

  • Patient accepts oral medications
  • Second-generation antipsychotic (Olanzapine) + Benzodiazepine

↓

LEVEL 3: PARENTERAL / INVOLUNTARY EMERGENCY PHARMACOLOGIC SEDATION

  • Severe Agitation / Violence Risk (RASS +3 to +4)
  • Intramuscular Sedation Protocols (Ketamine or Combination Agents)

Agitation Management Framework

Attempt verbal de-escalation first whenever safe and feasible. Use non-confrontational language, maintain an unblocked exit, establish a safe physical distance (>2 arm lengths), and minimize sensory overload.

Offer oral medications (e.g., Lorazepam 1–2 mg PO, Olanzapine 5– 10 mg ODT/PO) if the patient is cooperative.

Indicated when agitation poses an immediate threat to patient or staff safety, or impedes life-saving medical evaluation.

Section 5: Suicide Risk Stratification & Capacity Assessment

Suicide Risk Stratification Framework

All emergency department patients presenting with behavioral symptoms, mood disturbances, or self-harm must undergo structured suicide risk assessment.

HIGH RISK

  • Active plan & intent
  • Access to lethal means
  • Active psychosis or mania
  • Severe hopelessness

Action: Involuntary placement, 1:1 observation, secure room

MODERATE RISK

  • Ideation with plan, NO immediate intent
  • Risk factors present
  • Limited protective factors

Action: Full psych evaluation, safety planning, line of sight

LOW RISK

  • Passive ideation
  • No plan or intent
  • Strong protective factors
  • Cooperative with care

Action: Outpatient referral, lethal means safety counseling

High-Risk Indicators: Expressed active intent, detailed high-lethal plan, readily available lethal means (e.g., firearm in home), active psychosis, severe substance intoxication/withdrawal, history of high-lethal attempts, and lack of protective factors. Requires continuous 1:1 visual observation, removal of all personal belongings/ligature points, and involuntary psychiatric evaluation/admission.

Assessment of Decision-Making Capacity

Decision-making capacity is a clinical determination made by the emergency physician for a specific decision at a specific time. It is distinct from competence, which is a global legal status declared by a court.

4 Core Components of Decision-Making Capacity

  • Understanding: Ability to comprehend the nature of the condition, proposed treatment, and risks/benefits.
  • Appreciation: Ability to apply the information to one's own personal situation and recognize the reality of the medical consequences.
  • Reasoning: Ability to process choices logically and weigh risks versus benefits in alignment with personal values.
  • Communication: Ability to clearly communicate a consistent, voluntary choice.

CLINICAL WARNING: Intoxication, acute psychosis, severe hypoxia, or delirium automatically impairs decision-making capacity. If a patient lacking capacity attempts to refuse life-sustaining medical care, emergency physicians are legally required to provide care under the principle of implied consent or emergency protective custody.

Section 6: ED Behavioral Health Disposition Framework

ED disposition decisions require simultaneous confirmation of both medical stability and psychiatric safety.

ED Disposition Decision Tree

PATIENT EVALUATED IN ED

↓

MEDICAL SCREENING EXAM COMPLETE?

  • NO → Medical Admission (HHS, DKA, Sepsis) • YES → Is Patient Medically Stable?

↓

IS PATIENT MEDICALLY STABLE?

  • NO → Medical Admission
  • YES → Psychiatric Risk Assessment

↓

PSYCHIATRIC RISK ASSESSMENT

  • HIGH → Inpatient Psychiatric Admission
  • LOW → Outpatient Discharge with Safety Plan
Criteria for Inpatient Psychiatric Admission
  • Active suicidal or homicidal ideation with intent or plan.
  • Inability to care for basic self-needs ("grave disability") due to psychiatric illness.
  • Severe, uncompensated psychosis or mania posing safety threats.
  • Intractable drug/alcohol withdrawal requiring inpatient medical detoxification.
Criteria for Outpatient Behavioral Health Discharge
  • Medical screening examination complete and within normal limits.
  • Absence of active suicidal intent, plan, or homicidal ideation.
  • Reliable baseline mental status with demonstrated decision-making capacity.
  • Presence of a structured safety plan, safe home environment, lethal means restricted (firearms/medications locked), and secured outpatient follow-up within 24–48 hours.
